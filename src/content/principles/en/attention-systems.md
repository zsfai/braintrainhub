Many people picture attention as a battery that drains. A better image: **several subsystems cooperating**. The “can’t switch back” feeling after scrolling is often not total depletion, but rising control costs in a few subsystems.

## Three Common Attention Functions

1. **Alerting**: staying awake and ready to respond to signals.
2. **Orienting**: directing attention to a location, sound, or information channel.
3. **Executive control**: holding a goal amid conflict and interference, inhibiting impulsive responses.

In online training, [simple reaction time](/en/tools/reaction/) maps closely to alerting; [Schulte table](/en/tools/schulte/) trains orienting scans (full classic version at [Schulte table · schultetable.pro](https://schultetable.pro/)); [Stroop](/en/tools/stroop/) stresses executive control more.

## Why Short Video Taxes Switching

Short clips are fast, salient, and instantly rewarding. Orienting is pulled to each new stimulus; executive control wrestles between “one more” and “stop.” One switch looks cheap, but **high cumulative counts** make sustained tasks (reading, writing, work) feel sluggish.

## What Training Can and Cannot Do

Cognitive training tools help you **practice in controlled settings**: hold goals, resist distraction, stabilize responses. They are not treatment and cannot replace sleep, movement, or environment design. For a fuller path, see [Digital Environment Design](/en/methods/digital-environment/) and [21-Day Focus Rebuild](/en/plans/21-day-focus/).

## Summary

Treating attention as a system—not a battery—makes it easier to pick practice: stabilize responses for alerting, scan the field for orienting, resist interference for executive control.
